What we filtered on and why

We are not doctors or dieticians and this is not medical advice. What we do have is a database of 983+ protein bars with every macro tracked -- and we know what diabetics commonly prioritize when choosing a bar. So we filtered on those numbers, explained the reasoning behind each one, and let the data surface what qualifies.

One important note on our ingredient quality grade: the A through F score you see on every bar is our general assessment of ingredient quality. It measures how clean and minimally processed a bar's ingredients are. It is not a diabetic-specific score. A bar can score A on ingredient quality and still be high in sugar. A bar can score B because it contains glycerin or polydextrose -- which affects our general rating but has no meaningful impact on blood sugar. We show the grade here as additional context, not as a diabetic suitability ranking.

Sugar
5g or less
Sugar is the most direct driver of blood glucose spikes. It is listed plainly on the nutrition label. We set the ceiling at 5g -- tight enough to be meaningful, but not so tight that it eliminates every bar with a small amount of naturally occurring dairy sugar.
Net Carbs
10g or less
Net carbs = Total Carbs minus Fiber minus (Sugar Alcohols divided by 2). Fiber does not raise blood sugar. Sugar alcohols raise it at roughly half the rate of regular sugar, so they get a 50% credit in the formula. Net carbs gives a more accurate picture of glycemic load than total carbs alone. It is not printed on the label -- you calculate it, or use our numbers here.
Fiber
5g or more
Fiber slows gastric emptying, which means glucose from whatever carbs are in the bar enters the bloodstream more slowly. A bar with 8g of net carbs and 8g of fiber produces a meaningfully different blood sugar response than a bar with 8g net carbs and 1g of fiber. We required at least 5g. Many bars here hit 7-16g.
No Maltitol
Hard exclude
Maltitol is a sugar alcohol with a glycemic index of roughly 35 -- compared to 65 for table sugar. Food labeling rules allow brands to exclude it from the sugar line, so a bar can say 0g sugar while still containing maltitol. In our database, 40% of bars with 1g or less of listed sugar still contain maltitol. Every bar on this list has been checked and is maltitol-free.

Maltitol raises blood glucose. It just does not count toward the sugar line on the nutrition facts panel. Checking the ingredient list is the only way to know.
Barebells fails every bar on maltitol. All 25 Barebells bars in our database contain maltitol. They are popular and heavily marketed, but every single one is on the wrong side of the maltitol line.
Quest scores B on ingredient quality but qualifies cleanly. Quest bars contain erythritol and polydextrose -- which affect our general ingredient score -- but neither raises blood sugar meaningfully. Their fiber content (8-15g per bar) and low net carbs are exactly what this list is filtering for.
Fiber range is wide across qualifying bars. The 74 bars that qualify range from 5g to 16g of fiber. Higher is generally better for blood sugar management. The fiber source matters too -- chicory root and inulin are soluble fibers that slow glucose absorption more effectively than insoluble fiber sources.
IQ Bar leads on net carbs. Multiple IQ Bar flavors hit 2-3g of net carbs -- the lowest in the qualifying list. They use a fiber blend that keeps total carbs low while hitting the protein floor. Worth knowing if net carbs is your primary concern.
High ingredient quality score and diabetic-friendly are not the same thing. Some of the highest-scoring bars on ingredient quality do not appear here because they use date-based natural sugars that push them over the 5g sugar threshold. A clean ingredient list does not automatically mean blood-sugar-friendly.
Only 8% of bars in the database qualify. Out of 983+ bars, 74 meet all five criteria. Most bars -- including many marketed as healthy or high-protein -- do not pass the sugar and net carbs thresholds when maltitol is properly accounted for.

Frequently asked questions

Three things: sugar, net carbs, and fiber. Sugar directly raises blood glucose -- it is the most immediate concern. Net carbs gives a more accurate picture of total glycemic load by accounting for fiber and sugar alcohols. Fiber slows digestion and blunts glucose spikes. We set the bar at 5g or less sugar, 10g or less net carbs, and 5g or more fiber for every bar on this list.
Net carbs = Total Carbohydrates minus Fiber minus (Sugar Alcohols divided by 2). Fiber passes through without raising blood sugar. Sugar alcohols raise it at roughly half the rate of regular sugar, so they get a 50% credit. Net carbs is not printed on the nutrition label -- you have to calculate it. Every bar listing on this page shows the net carbs number we calculated directly from the database.
Fiber slows gastric emptying, which means whatever glucose comes from the carbs in a bar enters the bloodstream more gradually. Two bars with identical net carbs can produce meaningfully different blood sugar responses depending on their fiber content. We required at least 5g of fiber as a minimum. Several bars here hit 10-16g.
Maltitol is a sugar alcohol with a glycemic index of roughly 35, compared to 65 for regular table sugar. Food labeling rules let brands exclude it from the sugar line entirely. So a bar can say 0g sugar on the front of the package while containing maltitol, which still raises blood glucose. In our database, 40% of bars with 1g or less of listed sugar contain maltitol. Every bar on this list has been verified maltitol-free by checking the full ingredient list.
Our A through F grade is a general assessment of ingredient quality -- how clean and minimally processed the ingredients are. It is not a diabetic-specific score. A bar can score A on ingredient quality and still be high in sugar. A bar can score B because it contains glycerin or polydextrose, which affects our general rating but does not meaningfully affect blood sugar. We show it here as additional context. The primary filter for this list is sugar, net carbs, and fiber.
Quest bars contain erythritol and polydextrose -- ingredients that lower our general ingredient quality score. But neither raises blood sugar meaningfully. Quest's fiber content (8-15g depending on flavor) and consistently low net carbs are exactly the numbers this list filters on. The B grade reflects ingredient processing, not blood sugar impact.
RXBAR uses dates as their primary sweetener, which means natural sugar content is high -- most flavors have 12-16g of sugar. That pushes them well above our 5g sugar threshold. The ingredients are clean and whole-food, which is why they score well on our general ingredient quality grade. But clean ingredients and low blood sugar impact are different things for this category.
Out of 983+ bars, 74 meet all five criteria: 5g or less sugar, 10g or less net carbs, 5g or more fiber, 10g or more protein, A or B ingredient grade, and no maltitol. That is about 8% of the full database. Most bars -- including many marketed as healthy or diabetic-friendly -- do not pass when maltitol is properly accounted for and net carbs are calculated correctly.
