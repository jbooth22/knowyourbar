What We Found Analyzing 898 Protein Bars

We scored every ingredient in every bar. Here is what the data actually shows, without the marketing spin.

65%
of protein bars contain no artificial sweeteners and no sugar alcohols
The remaining 35% contain at least one: artificial sweeteners like sucralose or acesulfame, sugar alcohols like erythritol or maltitol, or both. That is most of the bars on the market.
21% of bars contain both artificial sweeteners and sugar alcohols in the same formula, chasing a low-sugar, high-protein profile using synthetic additives throughout.
Sucralose is the most common artificial sweetener, appearing in about 24% of all bars we track. Acesulfame potassium almost always appears alongside it.
Bars with 31+ ingredients average a quality score of 1.0. Bars with under 9 ingredients average 6.1. Shorter ingredient lists consistently score better.
Very high protein bars (25g+) average an ingredient quality score of just 1.9, the lowest of any protein tier. Maximizing protein often comes at an ingredient cost.
Even Grade A bars are not always clean. 27% of A-grade bars still contain artificial sweeteners or sugar alcohols. Grade alone is not enough to filter by.
26% of all bars have more than 10g of sugar per bar, including many marketed as healthy or natural options using fruit or honey rather than refined sugar.
What is in those 898 bars?
Sugar alcohols alone account for 11% of bars, and 21% use both artificial sweeteners and sugar alcohols together. Only 65% have neither.
More ingredients = lower quality score
Average ingredient quality score drops significantly as ingredient count rises. Bars with 31+ ingredients score more than 5 points lower than bars with under 9 ingredients.

Why most "clean protein bar" lists are not useful

Search "clean protein bars" and you will find list after list of the same bars, usually whatever is paying for affiliate placement that month. The word "clean" has no regulated definition, and brands use it freely on bars that contain sucralose, maltitol, palm kernel oil, and 25 other ingredients.

We took a different approach. Every bar in our database is scored based on its actual ingredient list: what ingredients appear, in what order, and what the research says about each one. A bar cannot buy its way onto this list. It either earns an A or B ingredient grade with no artificial sweeteners and no sugar alcohols, or it does not qualify.

Out of 898+ protein bars scored, 278 meet all three clean criteria. That is about 37% of the market. The other 63% contain artificial sweeteners, sugar alcohols, or both.

What the cleanest bars have in common

Looking across the top-scoring clean bars, a few patterns stand out consistently.

Protein comes first

In the highest-scoring bars, a quality protein source is the lead ingredient or appears within the first two positions. Whey protein isolate, egg whites, pea protein, nuts: whole or minimally processed sources that score well and put the most nutritionally significant ingredient right at the top.

Sweetness comes from real food

Dates, honey, fruit, and coconut sugar appear frequently. These are not zero-calorie solutions, and they do contribute natural sugars, which is why some of these bars score higher on total sugar than bars using erythritol. The difference is that these are whole food sources rather than synthetic compounds.

Short ingredient lists earn a bonus

Bars with fewer than eight ingredients get a small scoring bonus in our system because brevity usually reflects simplicity. The data backs this up: bars with under 9 ingredients average a score of 6.1, while bars with 31 or more average just 1.0.

The real tradeoff: protein and price

The cleanest bars tend to deliver less protein than bars using isolate-heavy formulas sweetened with sucralose. RXBAR gives you 12g from egg whites and nuts. If 20g or more is a firm requirement, the options narrow. But they exist: Atlas, Gryp, and PEAK Protein all hit 20g or more while meeting every clean criterion.

Ingredients to watch out for

Artificial sweeteners

Sucralose appears in about 24% of all bars we track. It shows up even in bars marketed as "natural." Acesulfame potassium almost always appears alongside sucralose. Aspartame is less common but still present in some. All three earn a negative score in our ingredient system.

Sugar alcohols

Maltitol has a higher glycemic impact than most sugar alcohols and is known to cause significant digestive distress. It scores -4 in our system. Erythritol is gentler on digestion but still synthetic and present in over 40 bars. Xylitol and sorbitol round out the most common ones. None appear in any bar on this list.

Processed oils

Palm kernel oil, fractionated palm oil, and partially hydrogenated oils frequently show up as coating agents. They drag ingredient scores down when they appear early in the list, though they are not in the same concern category as artificial sweeteners.

Frequently asked questions

What are the cleanest protein bars?

Based on ingredient quality scoring across 898+ bars, the cleanest protein bars are those earning an A grade with no artificial sweeteners and no sugar alcohols. Top options include RXBAR, Perfect Bar, IQ Bar, Munk Pack, and Atlas. Only about 65% of bars in our database meet all three clean criteria.

What protein bars have no artificial sweeteners?

Protein bars without artificial sweeteners include RXBAR, Perfect Bar, Larabar, GoMacro, IQ Bar, Simply Protein, and Munk Pack. The main sweeteners to avoid are sucralose, acesulfame potassium, aspartame, and saccharin. Sucralose alone appears in about 24% of all bars we track.

Are there protein bars without sugar alcohols?

Yes. RXBAR, Larabar, Perfect Bar, IQ Bar, Atlas, and GoMacro all avoid sugar alcohols entirely. Sweetness in these bars comes from dates, honey, or fruit. Sugar alcohols to watch for include erythritol, maltitol, xylitol, and sorbitol.

What makes a protein bar clean?

Our definition: an A or B ingredient grade, no artificial sweeteners, and no sugar alcohols. Of 898+ bars scored, 278 meet all three criteria. The word 'clean' has no regulated definition, so we use ingredient scoring to make it objective.
