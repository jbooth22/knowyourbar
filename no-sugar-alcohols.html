The 10 Best Protein Bars Without Sugar Alcohols

Sugar alcohols like maltitol, erythritol, sorbitol and xylitol are how most "low sugar" protein bars keep the sugar number down. For some people, they also cause bloating and stomach trouble. We screen for five of them, plus IMO, a fiber syrup that works the same way on a label.

Of the 1,000+ bars we track, 968 have none of them anywhere in the ingredient list. The other 26% do, and 108 of those still show 0g sugar alcohol on the nutrition label. That is why we check the ingredients, not just the label line.

What "no sugar alcohols" actually means

Sugar alcohols are the sweeteners bar makers reach for when they want a low sugar number on the label without giving up sweetness. This guide screens every bar for six of them: maltitol, erythritol, sorbitol, xylitol, isomalt, and isomalto-oligosaccharides (IMO), a prebiotic fiber syrup that behaves the same way on a label even though it isn't technically a sugar alcohol.

26% of the 1,000+ bars in our database still have a sugar alcohol on the label. Here is how often each one shows up, and where it usually hides.

Maltitol

If not sugar alcohols, then what?

Cutting sugar alcohols doesn't mean cutting sweetness. The 968 bars in this guide get their sweetness from two different places, and they show up very differently on the label.

These are real sugar. They add grams to the Sugars and Added Sugars lines just like the sugar in a candy bar would, they just come from a whole-food or less processed source instead of a lab.

Zero-calorie alternatives, won't trigger this screen either

These sweeteners are not sugar alcohols and don't get counted as sugar. They're the closest thing to a free pass: sweetness with no calories, no grams on the Sugars line, and none of the digestive baggage sugar alcohols carry.

Monk Fruit
122 bars13%
Zero calories, zero grams of sugar. Sweetness comes from mogrosides, not a sugar molecule, so it doesn't touch the sugar line or the sugar alcohol line.
Allulose
103 bars11%
A rare sugar that occurs naturally in small amounts in foods like figs. The FDA doesn't require it to be counted as sugar or added sugar, and it's not a sugar alcohol either.
Stevia
91 bars9%
Zero calories, zero sugar. Plant-derived, extracted from the stevia leaf rather than manufactured from a sugar molecule the way maltitol is.
Glycerin is a sugar alcohol gray area
  • Glycerin (glycerol) shows up in 355 of the 968 "no sugar alcohol" bars on this page (37%)
  • More than any single sugar alcohol we screen for
  • Glycerin is chemically a sugar alcohol, but we don't count it against a bar here

Here's why we don't include glycerin as a sugar alcohol

Glycerin (also listed as glycerol) is chemically a sugar alcohol and belongs to the same family as maltitol, erythritol, and xylitol. Using a chemistry definition, it belongs on this screen. (At least to the best of our understanding, as definitely NOT chemists.) However, we don't use it as a factor on our No Sugar Alcohols page. We'll do our best to show our work and explain why.

It's important to note that ingredients can serve more than one function. Maltitol is typically added as a sugar substitute, providing sweetness while keeping labeled sugar low. Glycerin is also a sugar alcohol and mildly sweet, but protein bars often use it as a humectant to keep the bar moist and soft. If you're like us and had no clue what humectant means, it's an ingredient that attracts and holds on to water or moisture. So an ingredient list doesn't necessarily tell us why an ingredient was added or which function matters most in the recipe, but its chemistry doesn't change based on intent.

We chose to use our best interpretation of FDA guidelines. The FDA's nutrition labeling rules treat glycerin differently from the sugar alcohols typically reported on Nutrition Facts labels. The regulations specifically address sugar alcohols such as sorbitol, mannitol, xylitol, maltitol, and erythritol. Glycerin is regulated separately, and the FDA recognizes it serves several functions. So for that reason, we're treating it differently too.

So what does that mean for glycerin on this No Sugar Alcohols page? Well, there are 71 brands we have listed on this page as not having sugar alcohols that have glycerin present. This includes several brands we think have very strong ingredient label quality. However, we understand if you have a different point of view and prefer to avoid glycerin. We've included a list of brands below that have glycerin used in all of their flavors and formulations.

Protein bars without erythritol

Erythritol is a fermented sugar alcohol made by fermenting glucose with a yeast-like fungus, roughly 70% as sweet as table sugar with close to zero calories. It has a glycemic index of 0, the lowest of any sugar alcohol on this page's screen, and is generally well tolerated at typical serving sizes, though some people still report bloating or a cooling aftertaste at higher doses.

99 of the 1,000+ bars we track, about 7.6%, contain erythritol. Screen for erythritol on its own, ignoring the other five sugar alcohols this page screens for, and the qualifying list gets bigger: 1,209 bars across 184 brands, 241 more than the 968 bars that clear this guide's full six-way sugar alcohol screen. The gap is bars that skip erythritol specifically but still contain something else on this list, most often maltitol or isomalto-oligosaccharides (IMO).

Erythritol and maltitol are not the same thing, and our Keto and Diabetics guides don't treat them the same way. Both guides exclude the maltitol family (maltitol, polyglycitol, hydrogenated starch hydrolysates) for its meaningfully higher glycemic index, around 35 versus sucrose's 65. Neither guide excludes erythritol, since its glycemic index of 0 already behaves the way their net-carbs formula assumes. A bar with erythritol can still qualify for Keto or Best Bars for Diabetics. A bar with maltitol cannot, even if that same bar happens to be erythritol-free.

Every bar in our Best 10 and Top 50 is erythritol-free, since they clear all six.

How we picked these

What qualifies: No maltitol, erythritol, sorbitol, xylitol, isomalt, or isomalto-oligosaccharides (IMO) anywhere in the ingredient list. We read the ingredient list itself, not just the Sugar Alcohol line on the label, because 108 bars name one and still show 0g. Glycerin does not count against a bar (see why). 968 of the 1,000+ bars we track qualify.

Rules for every pick:

  • The bar has to qualify for this guide, carry an A or B ingredient grade, and have at least 10g of protein.
  • We rank ingredient quality by grade only. Two bars with the same grade count as equal, because our scoring isn't precise enough to split them. When two bars tie on a pick's own number and grade, the one you can buy through a link on this page goes first (a brand's own partner link, then Amazon). After that, ties go to more protein, then less sugar, then more fiber, then fewer calories.
  • No bar appears twice in the Best 10, and no brand gets more than 2 of the 10 spots.
  • If a pick's rule finds no eligible bar, that spot goes to the next rule on this guide's backup list, so there are always 10.
  • Nobody pays for a spot. Every bar goes through the same rules. Buy links may earn us a commission. A link only ever decides between bars that are already tied, and never lifts a bar over one with a better grade or a better number.

How each pick was chosen:

  • Best overall: The best ingredient grade on the list, then the most protein per 100 calories, with 15g+ protein.
  • Cleanest ingredients: An A-grade bar (B if no A qualifies) with the shortest ingredient list. Every bar in the same grade is treated as equal on ingredient quality, so the tiebreak is a label you can count.
  • Highest protein: The most protein per bar, grade B or better, capped at 300 calories so a large candy-style bar cannot win on size alone.
  • Most protein per calorie: The most protein per 100 calories, grade B or better, 12g+ protein.
  • Lowest calorie: The best ingredient grade on the list, then the fewest calories, 10g+ protein.
  • Best from a big brand: The Best overall rule (best grade, then most protein per 100 calories), limited to brands with national grocery, big-box or Costco distribution. 10g+ protein.
  • Lowest sugar: The least sugar, grade B or better, 10g+ protein.
  • Best allulose-sweetened: Allulose on the label. Ranked with the Best overall rule (best grade, then most protein per 100 calories), 10g+ protein.
  • Best date-sweetened: Dates on the label, and no added sugar, syrup or sugar substitute. Ranked with the Best overall rule (best grade, then most protein per 100 calories), 10g+ protein.
  • Best plant-based: Vegan-labeled bars. Ranked with the Best overall rule (best grade, then most protein per 100 calories), 15g+ protein.

Frequently asked questions

We screen for Maltitol, Erythritol, Isomalto-oligosaccharides (IMO), Sorbitol, Xylitol, and Isomalt. These are the sugar alcohols and sugar-alcohol-adjacent sweeteners that actually show up in protein bar ingredient lists, not a generic definition. Any bar listing one of these gets flagged, regardless of how small the amount looks on the label.
Maltitol, sorbitol, and xylitol are well known for causing gas, bloating, or a laxative effect in some people, especially at the multi-gram doses common in low-sugar protein bars.
